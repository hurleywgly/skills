# Bottling, Finishing & Legal Guide

## Bottle Types

| Type | Closure | Best For | Considerations |
|------|---------|----------|----------------|
| **12oz beer bottles (brown)** | Crown caps | Most home ciders | Reusable, familiar size, requires capper |
| **22oz bomber bottles** | Crown caps | Sharing, gifts | Fewer bottles to fill |
| **750ml wine bottles** | Cork or screw-cap | Elegant presentation | Traditional feel, cork requires tool |
| **Swing-top (Grolsch style)** | Flip-top gasket | Rustic aesthetic, easy resealing | Gaskets degrade; not for pasteurization |
| **Champagne bottles** | Crown cap or cork + cage | Highly carbonated cider | Strongest glass for pressure |

**For beginners**: Start with 12oz brown beer bottles and crown caps. Familiar, affordable, and easy to share.

### Crown Cap System

- **Bottles**: Any standard beer bottle works. Brown glass protects from light.
- **Capper**: Two types—wing capper ($15-20) or bench capper ($30-50). Bench is easier.
- **Caps**: Standard 26mm crown caps. ~$5 for 144 caps.

### Swing-Top Bottles

- Convenient for home use (no tools needed to close)
- Gaskets need replacement over time
- Not recommended for high carbonation (pressure can pop tops)
- Don't use for pasteurization (rubber can't handle heat)

## Carbonation Methods

### Bottle Conditioning (Priming) — Recommended for Beginners

Natural carbonation using residual yeast and added sugar.

**Process**:
1. Confirm fermentation is complete (stable gravity for 3+ days)
2. Calculate priming sugar needed
3. Dissolve sugar in small amount of boiled water, cool
4. Gently mix sugar solution into cider (avoid splashing)
5. Bottle immediately
6. Store bottles at room temperature (65-75°F) for 2-3 weeks
7. Move to cool storage after carbonation develops

**Priming Sugar Amounts** (for 1 gallon):

| Carbonation Level | Sugar Amount |
|-------------------|--------------|
| Light (British style) | 0.5-1 oz (15-30g) |
| Medium | 1-1.5 oz (30-45g) |
| High (champagne style) | 1.5-2 oz (45-60g) |

**Rule of thumb**: 3-5 grams per 12oz bottle for medium carbonation.

**Sugar options**:
- Table sugar (sucrose): Clean, neutral, most common
- Honey: Adds subtle flavor complexity
- Apple juice concentrate: Maintains apple character

### Force Carbonation — More Advanced

Using a keg system and CO2 tank.

**Requirements**:
- Cornelius (corny) keg: $60-100
- CO2 tank: $50-100
- Regulator: $40-80
- Tubing and disconnects: $20-40

**Process**:
1. Transfer dry cider to sanitized keg
2. Seal and purge headspace with CO2
3. Set regulator to 10-12 PSI
4. Store cold (35-40°F) for 5-7 days
5. Cider absorbs CO2 from headspace

**Advantages**:
- Consistent carbonation level
- Faster than bottle conditioning
- No sediment in bottles
- Can adjust carbonation after the fact

**For beginners**: Wait until you're making 5+ gallon batches regularly before investing in kegging.

## Back-Sweetening (If Desired)

True dry cider has no residual sweetness. But if you want slight sweetness:

### Option 1: Stabilize + Sweeten

1. Confirm fermentation is complete
2. Add potassium metabisulfite (50 ppm) AND potassium sorbate (1g/L or 1/2 tsp per gallon)
3. Wait 24 hours for stabilizers to work
4. Add sweetener to taste (start small, you can always add more)
5. Bottle still (no carbonation) or force carbonate

**Warning**: You cannot bottle condition after adding sorbate. The yeast is inhibited. Use force carbonation or accept still cider.

### Option 2: Non-Fermentable Sweeteners

These won't restart fermentation:
- Erythritol: Clean sweetness, slight cooling sensation
- Xylitol: Sweet, slight cooling
- Stevia: Intensely sweet, can taste artificial in cider
- Sucralose (Splenda): Works but can taste off

**Best approach for dry cider**: Don't back-sweeten. If your cider tastes too tart or harsh, that's an apple selection or fermentation issue to address in the next batch.

## Bottling Process

### Pre-Bottling Checklist

- [ ] Fermentation complete (stable gravity 3+ days)
- [ ] Cider cleared (visible sediment settled)
- [ ] Final rack completed (cider off heavy lees)
- [ ] Bottles cleaned and sanitized
- [ ] Caps/closures ready
- [ ] Priming sugar calculated and dissolved (if carbonating)
- [ ] Sulfite added for preservation (optional but recommended: 30-50 ppm)

### Step-by-Step

1. **Sanitize**: Bottles, caps, siphon, filling equipment, bottling bucket

2. **Add priming sugar** (if bottle conditioning):
   - Dissolve in 1/2 cup boiled water
   - Cool to room temperature
   - Add to bottling bucket FIRST

3. **Transfer cider to bottling bucket**:
   - Siphon gently to avoid oxygen
   - Let priming sugar mix naturally during transfer

4. **Fill bottles**:
   - Use bottle filler or siphon
   - Fill to 1 inch below top (headspace needed)
   - Work quickly to minimize oxygen exposure

5. **Cap/seal**:
   - Crown caps: Use capper, ensure tight seal
   - Swing-tops: Check gasket condition
   - Corks: Use corker, consider wax seal

6. **Label** (optional but recommended):
   - Batch date
   - Apple varieties used
   - ABV (if calculated)
   - Tasting notes

### Post-Bottling

- **For bottle conditioning**: Store at 65-75°F for 2-3 weeks
- **For still cider**: Store cool (50-60°F) immediately
- **Long-term**: Cool, dark storage extends shelf life
- **Drink window**: 6 months to 2 years for most dry ciders

## Label Design

### What to Include

**Required** (if you ever share):
- What it is ("Dry Apple Cider")
- Batch date or vintage
- Your name/farm name

**Helpful**:
- Apple varieties used
- ABV percentage
- Tasting notes
- Serving suggestions

### DIY Label Options

| Method | Cost | Quality |
|--------|------|---------|
| Avery mailing labels + inkjet printer | $0.05/label | Basic but functional |
| Photo paper + color laser | $0.10/label | Better appearance |
| Canva templates + office printer | $0.05-0.15/label | Professional look, easy design |
| Professional print services | $0.50-2/label | Highest quality |

### Design Tips

- **Size**: 3" × 4" to 4" × 5" fits most 12oz bottles
- **Paper**: Waterproof or apply clear coat (condensation will destroy paper labels)
- **Style**: Homebrew culture appreciates humor, heritage, and honesty
- **Inspiration**: Browse 99designs.com/inspiration/labels/cider for ideas

## Washington State Legal Framework

### Home Cider Making: LEGAL

Washington state permits home fermentation for personal use:

- **No license required** for personal consumption
- **Can transport**: Up to 20 gallons per year can leave your home
- **Can share**: Legal to give away; you just can't sell

### What You CANNOT Do

| Activity | Legal Status |
|----------|--------------|
| Sell any amount | Illegal without license |
| Give as business samples | Illegal (commercial use) |
| Distill into spirits | Federal crime |
| Make more than reasonable personal use | Gray area—no specific limit but "personal use" must be believable |

### Federal TTB Considerations

The Alcohol and Tobacco Tax and Trade Bureau (TTB) has thresholds that affect cider:

| ABV Level | Classification | Implications |
|-----------|----------------|--------------|
| **Under 7% ABV** | "Hard cider" (favorable) | No federal permit for home production |
| **7% ABV or higher** | Treated as wine | Subject to wine regulations |

**Practical advice**: Keep your cider under 7% ABV to avoid federal complexity. This is typical for dry cider from 11-12 Brix juice anyway.

### Resources

- Washington Homebrewers Association legal info: homebrewersassociation.org/homebrewing-rights/statutes/washington/
- TTB cider resources: ttb.gov/cider/cider-resources

## Storage & Shelf Life

### Ideal Storage Conditions

| Factor | Ideal | Acceptable |
|--------|-------|------------|
| Temperature | 50-55°F | 45-65°F |
| Light | Dark | Minimal light (brown bottles help) |
| Position | On side (if corked) or upright | Either for crown caps |
| Humidity | 60-70% (if corked) | Not critical for caps |

### Shelf Life

| Storage | Shelf Life |
|---------|------------|
| Cool, dark cellar | 2-3 years |
| Room temperature | 6-12 months |
| Refrigerated | 1+ years |

**Dry cider ages well**: Unlike sweet ciders, dry ciders improve with age (up to a point). Tannins soften, flavors integrate, and complexity develops.

### Signs of Spoilage

- Vinegar smell (acetobacter contamination)
- Sulfur smell that doesn't dissipate (yeast stress, might improve with air)
- Visible mold
- Cloudiness in previously clear cider (possible refermentation or contamination)
- Off-putting flavors that don't match expectations

**If in doubt**: Don't drink it. Your nose knows.

# Apple Selection & Blending for Dry Cider

## The Core Principle

Few single apple varieties make excellent dry cider alone. Great dry cider comes from blending varieties to achieve balance across three dimensions:

- **Sugar** — Provides fermentation fuel and body
- **Acid** — Provides brightness, refreshment, and fermentation protection
- **Tannin** — Provides structure, complexity, and mouthfeel

---

## The Long Ashton Classification

Developed in 1903, this system categorizes cider apples by acid and tannin levels:

### Bittersweet (High tannin, low acid)
The backbone of traditional English cider. Provides body, structure, and complexity.

**Examples**:
- Dabinett (arguably the most versatile cider apple)
- Yarlington Mill
- Tremlett's Bitter
- Michelin
- Chisel Jersey

**Role in blend**: 40-60% for rich, structured dry cider

### Bittersharp (High tannin AND acid)
The "perfect" cider apples—can often produce excellent cider alone.

**Examples**:
- Kingston Black (legendary single-varietal cider apple)
- Foxwhelp
- Stoke Red
- Porter's Perfection

**Role in blend**: 20-40% for complexity and balance

### Sharp (High acid, low tannin)
Provides tartness and fermentation protection.

**Examples**:
- Granny Smith
- Bramley's Seedling
- Brown's Apple

**Role in blend**: 10-30% to brighten and balance

### Sweet (Low acid, low tannin)
Provides fermentable sugar and approachable flavor.

**Examples**:
- Gala
- Fuji
- Golden Delicious
- Most common grocery store varieties

**Role in blend**: 20-40% as base

---

## The Acid-Tannin-Sugar Triangle

For balanced dry cider, aim for:

| Component | Target Range | What It Does |
|-----------|--------------|--------------|
| **Sugar (Brix)** | 11-14% | Determines potential alcohol (1% Brix ≈ 0.6% ABV) |
| **Acid** | 0.4-0.6% | Creates refreshment; below 0.3% tastes flat |
| **Tannin** | 0.1-0.2% | Provides grip and structure; above 0.3% can be harsh |

**The relationship**: In dry cider (no residual sugar), acid and tannin must balance each other. Without sweetness to mask them, both become more prominent.

---

## Brix and ABV

**Brix** measures sugar percentage in juice. Most apples produce juice between 10-14 Brix.

**Conversion**: 1% Brix ≈ 0.6% ABV when fully fermented

| Starting Brix | Expected ABV |
|---------------|--------------|
| 10% | ~6.0% |
| 11% | ~6.6% |
| 12% | ~7.2% |
| 13% | ~7.8% |
| 14% | ~8.4% |

**For dry cider**: 11-13 Brix is ideal. Lower produces thin cider; higher can stress yeast.

**Measuring**: Use a refractometer (best) or hydrometer with your juice before pitching.

## Blending Strategies

### Simple Beginner Blend (Using Grocery Apples)

If you're starting with store-bought apples or juice, aim for:

- **60% sweet base**: Gala, Fuji, or Honeycrisp
- **30% sharp**: Granny Smith or Pink Lady
- **10% tannin addition**: Either crabapples or add tannin powder (available at homebrew shops)

This won't match traditional cider apple blends, but produces drinkable dry cider.

### Intermediate Blend (Mixed Sources)

Combine grocery apples with cider-specific varieties from orchards:

- **40% bittersweet**: Dabinett or Yarlington Mill (from specialty orchards)
- **30% sweet**: Honeycrisp or Gala
- **20% sharp**: Granny Smith
- **10% bittersharp**: Kingston Black or Foxwhelp if available

### Traditional English Style

For rich, complex dry cider:

- **50-60% bittersweet**: Dabinett, Yarlington Mill, Michelin
- **20-30% bittersharp**: Kingston Black, Foxwhelp
- **10-20% sharp**: For acid balance

### French (Normandy) Style

For drier, more tannic character:

- **60-70% bittersweet varieties**
- **20-30% bittersharp**
- **Minimal sharp** (French ciders tolerate lower acid)

## Wild and Foraged Apples

The Pacific Northwest has strong foraging traditions. Wild apples from abandoned orchards can produce excellent cider.

### Advantages
- Free fruit
- Often high in tannins (natural seedlings tend toward wild character)
- Unique local terroir
- Part of PNW cider culture (Greenwood Cider, Finnriver both use foraged fruit)

### Considerations
- Identification skills required
- Tannin and acid levels are unpredictable
- Blend with known varieties for consistency
- Press small test batches first

### Where to Find
- Old homestead orchards throughout Western Washington
- Abandoned farms on Olympic Peninsula
- Urban neighborhoods with mature trees
- Road rights-of-way (with landowner permission)

**Practical approach**: Foraged apples work best as 20-30% of a blend with known cider varieties, adding complexity without unpredictability.

## Single-Varietal vs. Blended

### Single-Varietal Cider

Only a few apples produce excellent dry cider alone:

- **Kingston Black** — The gold standard. Balanced bittersharp.
- **Dabinett** — Can work solo but benefits from acid addition.
- **Golden Russet** — Traditional American single-varietal.

### When to Blend

Almost always. Blending:
- Compensates for variety weaknesses
- Creates complexity impossible from one variety
- Allows use of available fruit
- Follows centuries of cider tradition

**Rule of thumb**: Unless you have Kingston Black or exceptional heritage varieties, blend.

## Practical Sourcing for Seattle Area

### For Best Dry Cider

1. **Primary source**: Finnriver or specialty orchards for bittersweet/bittersharp varieties (40-60% of blend)
2. **Secondary source**: U-pick orchards or farmers markets for heritage sharps (20-30%)
3. **Backup source**: Grocery store Granny Smith for acid balance (10-20%)

### For Acceptable First Batch

1. Fresh-pressed juice from local co-op or orchard (pre-blended)
2. Add tannin powder to compensate for culinary apple base
3. Ensure juice is unpasteurized (or pasteurized without preservatives)

### What to Avoid

- Juice with potassium sorbate (prevents fermentation)
- Juice with ascorbic acid (vitamin C—acceptable but affects flavor)
- Red Delicious apples (bland, mealy)
- Overripe or bruised fruit (introduces acetobacter)

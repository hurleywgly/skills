# How To Talk So Kids Will Listen & Listen So Kids Will Talk

**Book**: How To Talk So Kids Will Listen & Listen So Kids Will Talk by Adele Faber & Elaine Mazlish
**Why You Love It**: Simple, practical communication scripts that work for business AND parenting. Focus on cooperation, validating feelings, and consequences instead of control.

---

## Core Philosophy

**The Big Shift**: From controlling children to engaging cooperation

**Foundation**: Children cooperate when they feel:
- Heard and understood
- Respected as individuals
- Part of the solution, not the problem

---

## The 5 Skills to Encourage Cooperation

### 1. Describe What You See (or the Problem)

**Instead of attacking the child, describe the problem objectively.**

| Don't Say | Do Say |
|---|---|
| "You're so messy! Clean this up!" | "I see toys all over the floor." |
| "You never listen!" | "I notice the TV is still on after I asked you to turn it off." |
| "Why can't you be more careful?!" | "The milk spilled on the table." |

**Why It Works**: Shifts from child feeling attacked to seeing an opportunity to problem-solve.

---

### 2. Give Information

**Explain WHY the social or family norm exists.**

| Situation | Give Information |
|---|---|
| Food left out | "Leaving food out encourages ants." |
| Writing on walls | "Walls are not for writing on. Paper is for writing on." |
| Shoes on furniture | "Shoes carry dirt that damages the couch." |

**Why It Works**: Kids can act on information. They can't act on blame or criticism.

---

### 3. Say It With One Word

**It's easier for kids to hear one word than a lecture.**

| Instead of Lecture | One Word |
|---|---|
| "I can't believe you left your backpack on the floor again! How many times do I have to tell you..." | "Backpack!" |
| "We're about to leave and you're not even dressed yet! This is ridiculous..." | "Clothes!" |
| "You know the dishwasher needs to be loaded and..." | "Dishes!" |

**Important**: The word should NOT be the child's name or just "NO" - both fail to give enough information.

---

### 4. Describe Your Feelings (vs Attacking Character)

**Talk about YOUR feelings, not their character defects.**

| Attacking Character | Describing Feelings |
|---|---|
| "You're so irresponsible!" | "I feel frustrated when I trip over shoes in the hallway." |
| "You're such a slob!" | "I get overwhelmed when I see dishes piled in the sink." |
| "You don't care about anyone but yourself!" | "I feel hurt when plans change without letting me know." |

**Formula**: "I feel _____ when _____" (focus on behavior, not person)

---

### 5. Write a Note

**Sometimes written communication breaks through when verbal doesn't.**

**Examples**:
- Note on bathroom mirror: "Please hang up wet towels - they get mildewy on the floor!"
- Note on TV: "Homework first, then screen time"
- Note in lunchbox: "You've got this today!"

**Why It Works**: Novelty gets attention, removes nagging tone, can be playful.

## Helping Children Deal With Feelings

### The Problem

When kids express emotions, our instincts often make it worse:
- "Don't cry, it's not that bad!"
- "You don't really feel that way."
- "Stop being dramatic!"

**Result**: Kids feel misunderstood, dismissed, alone with big feelings.

### The Solution: Acknowledge Feelings

**Step 1: Listen with full attention** (put down phone, make eye contact)

**Step 2: Acknowledge with words**
- "Oh." / "Mmm." / "I see."
- This shows you're listening

**Step 3: Name the feeling**
- "That sounds frustrating."
- "You seem really disappointed."
- "You're angry that happened."

**Step 4: Give them their wish in fantasy** (when you can't in reality)
- "I wish I could make the park open right now!"
- "Wouldn't it be great if you could have dessert for every meal?"

### Why Naming Feelings Works

> "The book teaches a way to talk that names emotions and acknowledges the emotions that often a child cannot articulate."

When you name what they're feeling:
- They feel understood
- It helps them process and regulate
- Big feelings become manageable

**Example**:

| Dismissing | Acknowledging |
|---|---|
| Child: "I hate my brother!" / Parent: "No you don't, you love him." | Child: "I hate my brother!" / Parent: "You're really angry at him right now. Tell me what happened." |
| Child: "This is too hard!" / Parent: "It's not that hard, just try." | Child: "This is too hard!" / Parent: "This feels challenging. What part is tricky?" |

## Alternatives to Punishment

### The Problem with Punishment

- Focuses on past behavior
- Creates resentment and defiance
- Doesn't teach better behavior
- Damages relationship

### Better Approaches

#### 1. **Point Out a Way to Be Helpful**
- "The dog needs water in her bowl."
- "The door is open and cold air is coming in."

#### 2. **Express Strong Disapproval (Without Attacking Character)**
- "I don't like seeing the wall marked up!" (not "You're destructive!")
- "I'm very upset about this!" (describes YOUR feeling)

#### 3. **State Your Expectations**
- "I expect the homework to be done before screen time."
- "I expect wet towels to be hung up."

#### 4. **Show How to Make Amends**
- "Let's figure out how to clean this up together."
- "Your sister is upset. What could help her feel better?"

#### 5. **Give a Choice**
- "You can walk holding my hand or ride in the cart. You choose."
- "Jacket on or jacket carried. What works for you?"

#### 6. **Let Natural Consequences Happen** (when safe)
- Forgot lunch? They're hungry at school. (Don't rescue.)
- Didn't put laundry in hamper? Favorite shirt isn't clean.

## Enforcing Consequences

**When boundaries are crossed, consequences should be:**

1. **Related to the behavior**
   - Broke a toy by being rough → No more toy
   - Threw food → Meal is over

2. **Respectful** (not punitive or shaming)
   - Calm tone
   - Matter-of-fact
   - "Because X happened, now Y"

3. **Reasonable** (fits the "crime")
   - Not extreme or arbitrary
   - Age-appropriate

**Example**:
- **Behavior**: Hitting sibling
- **Consequence**: "Hitting hurts. You need space to calm down. You can rejoin us when you're ready to be gentle."

## Encouraging Autonomy

### Give Choices

**Let them make age-appropriate decisions:**
- "Red shirt or blue shirt?"
- "Park or library today?"
- "Carrots or broccoli with dinner?"

**Why**: Builds decision-making, sense of control, cooperation

### Don't Rush to Help

**Let them struggle (appropriately):**
- Zipping jacket
- Pouring milk (even if some spills)
- Solving a puzzle

**Why**: Competence builds confidence

### Respect Their Struggles

**Don't minimize or dismiss:**
- "That does sound tricky."
- "Looks like you're working hard on that."

## Communication Approach

> "An assertive way of communicating is **firm, consistent, clear, positive, warm, and confident**."

**Firm**: Boundaries are clear and held
**Consistent**: Same rules apply every time
**Clear**: No ambiguity about expectations
**Positive**: Focus on what TO do, not just what NOT to do
**Warm**: Respectful, kind tone
**Confident**: You believe in the boundary

---

## Making It About Behavior, Not Child

> "Making correcting behavior about the behavior, and not about the child."

| About the Child (Shame) | About the Behavior (Teach) |
|---|---|
| "You're so careless!" | "The cup fell and broke." |
| "You're mean!" | "That hurt your sister's feelings." |
| "You're irresponsible!" | "The homework didn't get done." |

**Why It Matters**: Shame creates defensiveness. Behavior focus creates opportunity to learn.

## Practical Scripts for Common Situations

### Mealtime

| Situation | Script |
|---|---|
| Won't sit at table | "Dinner is ready. Bodies at the table, please." (describe + one word) |
| Playing with food | "Food is for eating. If you're done, let me know." (information + choice) |
| Refuses to eat | "This is what we're having. You don't have to eat it, but this is dinner." (state expectation, no force) |

### Bedtime

| Situation | Script |
|---|---|
| Stalling | "It's 7:30. Bedtime routine starts." (information) |
| Wants one more story | "We read two stories. Now it's time to sleep." (state fact) |
| Won't stay in bed | "Your body needs rest. I'll check on you in a few minutes." (information + connection) |

### Screen Time

| Situation | Script |
|---|---|
| Tantrum when time's up | "I know you want to keep watching. Screen time is over now." (acknowledge feeling + firm boundary) |
| Negotiating for more | "The timer went off. Screen goes away now." (fact, consequence) |

### Tantrums

| Situation | Script |
|---|---|
| Public meltdown | "You're really upset. I'm right here." (acknowledge + presence) |
| Can't have something | "You wish you could have that. It's not happening today." (wish in fantasy + firm boundary) |

## Key Takeaways

1. **Cooperation comes from feeling respected**, not controlled
2. **Describe, don't attack** - Talk about the situation, not the child's character
3. **Acknowledge feelings** - Name emotions to help kids process them
4. **Give information** - Kids can act on facts
5. **Consequences, not punishment** - Related, respectful, reasonable
6. **Choices build autonomy** - Let them decide when possible
7. **One word > lecture** - Less talking, more impact

---

## How This Fits Your Philosophy

- **Actions > words**: Use fewer, more intentional words
- **Calm begets calm**: Assertive but warm communication
- **Cooperation over conflict**: These scripts build teamwork, not power struggles
- **Model behavior**: Respectful communication teaches respect

**Bottom Line**: You're teaching them HOW to communicate, solve problems, and cooperate - not just forcing compliance.
